Chirandu Farms · AI Sense camera review · 17 Aug – 20 Sep 2026

How often did deer come to Chirandu?

We went through every video the farm's cameras recorded over five weeks: 41,729 short HD clips. Every clip that showed anything animal-shaped was checked by eye.

In five weeks the cameras caught one deer visit: two deer grazing in the vegetable field by the greenhouses at first light on Saturday 19 September, 6:32–6:35 am. None came past the two corn cameras. Foxes were the regular visitor, on 21 of the 35 nights.

The one deer visit

Veggies camera, Saturday 19 Sep 2026. These are the camera's own HD recordings (1080p), about 3 minutes in total.

When each camera was recording

The cameras didn't all run the whole time. The gate camera never sent a video. Both corn cameras stopped on the morning of 12 September, and the whole system went offline on 21 September, so the last week isn't covered.

What else came through

Foxes were the steady visitor, almost always between 9 pm and 6 am, with a burst around 5 am. Raccoons, groundhogs, cats, rabbits and a skunk showed up a handful of times each. People working in the fields account for the daytime triggers.

What set the cameras off

Of the 41,729 clips, about 9,000 had something the software thought was an animal. We grouped those into 3,419 events and checked 2,006 of them by eye. The rest were one fixed object seen over and over in the same spot; we spot-checked those too. Almost everything was insects, fixed objects, people or birds.

So where and when would a sound pod help?

  1. Ask where the damage is before placing anything. In five weeks only one deer visit crossed these cameras, so either deer are light here right now, or they come in where the cameras aren't looking. If the farm sees browsing or tracks somewhere specific, that's where the cameras and the sound pod belong.
  2. If one pod goes in now, put it at the vegetable field by the greenhouses. That's the only spot deer were seen, in the middle of the Veggies camera's view. Aim it across the field toward the tree line they came from.
  3. Run it from dusk until about 7:30 am, with the most attention 5:30–7:30 am. The only visit was at first light, and deer here are most active at dawn and dusk.
  4. It must only react to deer. Nearly every trigger was an insect in the night light, a fixed object, a bird or a person. A speaker set off by motion alone would sound all night and teach deer to ignore it.
  5. Get the cameras back and keep them running into November. Fix the gate camera, restart the two corn cameras, and bring the system back online. Deer movement picks up in October–November as the rut starts and the corn comes out, so the next weeks will say far more than late summer did.

How we checked

Every clip was screened. We ran the same animal-detection models the AI Sense platform uses (MegaDetector and SpeciesNet) over all 41,729 HD clips from the Veggies, Corn right and Corn left cameras, two frames per second. 51 clips were damaged and couldn't be read.

Every possible animal was looked at. Clips with anything animal-shaped were grouped into events a few minutes long. 2,006 events were reviewed by eye on full-resolution frames. Every event called "deer" or "unclear" got a second, independent look aimed at ruling it out. The two night-time calls that survived were then checked frame by frame and turned out to be moths close to the lens.

We looked for deer we might have missed. We checked a further 300 clips the review never saw: 154 random clips where the software found nothing (mostly night, dusk and dawn), 145 of the "fixed object" events, and the one leftover clip the software had labelled as deer. None had a deer.

Limits. Cameras only see their own view. The gate camera never recorded, the corn cameras stopped on 12 September, and nothing after 20 September is covered. A deer standing very far off in the dark could still slip past the software, but the one real visit was picked up with high confidence.
